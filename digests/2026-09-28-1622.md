## 1. Symbiotic Architecture for Post-Hoc Audio Extension of Frozen Language Models — `2609.30784` — **Relevance 10/10 · Quality 7/10**

*Yotaro Kubo, Qi Sun, Yujin Tang* — **Sakana AI**, Tokyo · cs.SD, cs.CL, eess.AS · v1 submitted 2026-09-25 · submitted to ICASSP · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.30784)

*Problem:* Turning an LLM into an audio LLM by fine-tuning it destroys its text ability, and audio prefilling costs scale with the full backbone width.
*Method:* An "injector" module writes audio-conditioned key/value vectors straight into the frozen backbone's KV cache, bypassing the LLM entirely during audio ingestion; noisy-RoPE training and KV scale matching stabilise it.
*Result:* Approaches a fine-tuned ALM on ASR, audio QA and scene classification while activating 552M instead of 1,108M parameters at prefill, and preserves text-only scores exactly, by construction.

**Why it ranked:** Relevance 10 — this is Primary Focus #4 stated as an architecture: "speech–text alignment such that the backbone LLM loses the least knowledge", with `catastrophic forgetting` literally in its index terms. It also hands you the number that makes the case: audio instruction-tuning drops **GSM8K from 57.8 to 2.0**, HellaSwag from 47.3 to 38.2, and WikiText-2 BPB from 0.88 to 1.32 — the forgetting tax quantified on a reasoning benchmark, not just on perplexity. The long-utterance ablation is the other thing worth having: without noisy RoPE, WER on held-out >25 s utterances is **120.68%**; with it, 10.49%; with KV scale matching too, 4.70% — a clean demonstration that KV injection breaks on unseen lengths unless you train for it. Quality held at 7, not higher: the scale is small (a ~1B-class backbone, LibriSpeech + Clotho-AQA + CochlScene only), there is no comparison against any published ALM — "Monolithic" is their own fine-tune, so "approaches a fine-tuned ALM" means approaches *theirs* — the CochlScene gap is acknowledged but unexplained, no code or weights are mentioned, and "preserves text performance by construction" is a tautology rather than a finding. It is a five-page ICASSP submission with one good idea and honest ablations, which is exactly what it should be judged as.

## 2. ★ All In Good Time: Causality-Aware Framework for LLM-Based Simultaneous Speech-to-Speech Translation — `2609.30416` — **Relevance 9/10 · Quality 8/10**

*Amir Hussein, Enas Albasiri, Travis M. Bartley, Nourchene Ferchichi, Ke Hu, Harishchandra Dubey, Myungjong Kim, Zhehuai Chen, Oluwatobi Olabiyi, Sanjeev Khudanpur* — **Johns Hopkins University** and **NVIDIA** · cs.CL · v1 submitted 2026-09-24 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.30416)

*Problem:* Simul-S2ST lacks causally aligned training data with cross-lingual speaker fidelity, and existing systems use fixed read/write policies or confidence heuristics.
*Method:* A data pipeline generating causally aligned segments with voice transfer, plus a factorized architecture (FAST), a causality-aware adaptive policy (CAP), and a causality-aware latency metric (CAAL).
*Result:* +1.2 BLEU and 26% lower latency over a fixed policy on CVSS es/de/fr; against Hibiki-Zero, +2.3 BLEU and −38.8% CAAL latency on 8K training hours versus its 160K.

**Why it ranked:** Relevance 9 — simultaneous speech translation is Primary Focus #5, and this is the whole stack (data, architecture, policy, metric) rather than one component. Quality 8 because the headline comparison is the hard one and they win it on a 20× data disadvantage: **8K hours against Hibiki-Zero's 160K and SeamlessM4T's 145K/245K**, yet +2.3 BLEU / +1.3 chrF++ / +3.2 COMET over Hibiki-Zero with comparable speaker similarity, and higher speaker similarity than SeamlessM4T (0.44 vs 0.31) at 30.9% lower latency. The **CAAL metric is the contribution I would actually reuse** — standard average lagging credits systems that peek ahead, and a causality-aware variant is the right correction for a field that reports latency as a scalar. They also state their remaining gap plainly (generated *speech* quality, not text translation) instead of burying it, and the FAST-CAP-L variant shows what closes it. Held at 8: the SOTA comparison required swapping in a 0.6B NVIDIA encoder and enlarging the chunk to 2 s, so the headline model is not the ablated one; CVSS is synthetic-target TTS data, so speaker-similarity numbers are flattered relative to real speech; three European target languages only; no code release stated; and CAAL is a metric the authors both propose and win under, which is worth a second look before trusting it.

## 3. ★ YODAS v3: Over 1 Million Hours of High-Bandwidth, Stereophonic, Multilingual Speech — `2609.29448` — **Relevance 8/10 · Quality 9/10**

*William Chen, Shinnosuke Takamichi, Sayaka Shiota, Satoru Fukayama, Samuele Cornell, Shinji Watanabe* — **Carnegie Mellon University**, **Keio University**, **Tokyo Metropolitan University**, **AIST** · cs.CL · v1 submitted 2026-09-24 · Interspeech 2026 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29448)

*Problem:* Open large-scale speech corpora are 16 kHz, mono, and English-skewed, so they cannot support high-fidelity or multi-channel research or genuinely balanced multilingual training.
*Method:* A keyword-generation and video-search crawl pipeline explicitly designed to balance languages, followed by bandwidth, channel and transcription-quality analysis, then baseline ASR and neural-codec training.
*Result:* 1.1M hours of 48 kHz multi-channel audio in 147 languages under CC BY 3.0 — 22 languages over 10K hours, 73 over 5K — and usable unfiltered.

**Why it ranked:** Relevance 8 — massively multilingual and low-resource ASR data is Primary Focus #2, and the language balance is the part that matters: 73 languages past 5K hours is a different resource from "one big English corpus plus a tail". Quality 9, the highest here, because this is the rare paper whose claim is checkable by downloading it, and because the ASR experiment answers the question a practitioner actually has. Training OWSM-v4-base on seven languages across four CTC-score filtering thresholds, **WER improves almost monotonically as filtering gets *looser*** — best results at θ=0.00 (no filtering) for six of seven languages — which inverts YODAS v2's behaviour, where insufficient filtering produced 100+ WER divergence. That is a strong, falsifiable statement that the transcripts are clean enough to use out of the box, and it is worth more than any single WER. It is also Interspeech-accepted (so peer-reviewed, unlike everything else in this digest), CC BY 3.0, CC-Zero licensed on arXiv, and already on the Hub at `espnet/yodas3`. Docked one point only because it is a resource paper — the baselines are deliberately small (102M, 40K steps, 7 languages of 147), the transcripts are weak labels from YouTube captions with the provenance and bias that implies, and no model trained on the full 1.1M hours is released to prove the scale pays.

## 4. ★ Agentic-GER: Terminology Recovery in Long-Form Speech Using Global Context — `2609.29428` — **Relevance 9/10 · Quality 7/10**

*Yanqiao Zhu, Wupeng Wang, Zhifu Gao, Xiangang Li, Xie Chen* — **X-LANCE Lab, Shanghai Jiao Tong University**, **Shanghai Innovation Institute**, **Alibaba Token Foundry** · cs.CL, cs.AI · v1 submitted 2026-09-24 · submitted to ICASSP 2027 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.29428)

*Problem:* Long-form ASR transcribes domain terminology inconsistently, and per-segment post-correction has no view of the terms established elsewhere in the same recording.
*Method:* An LLM agent reads the whole transcript to flag suspicious terms, selectively re-transcribes those audio spans to test candidate corrections, and feeds accepted edits forward to constrain later decisions.
*Result:* B-CER/B-WER improve for every one of four LLMs under two ASR systems on GigaSpeechBench; Whisper Chinese B-CER falls 35.07% → 22.16%, a 36.8% relative cut.

**Why it ranked:** Relevance 9 — entity-aware and rare-word recognition is named explicitly in Primary Focus #4, and "use the LLM's world knowledge plus global context to fix terminology" is that item's natural attack. The ablation is what earns the Quality 7 rather than a 6: against **local GER** — same model, same ASR hypothesis, one shot per segment, no global context — local GER nudges B-CER 10.91 → 10.63 *while making overall CER worse* (3.12 → 3.42), whereas Agentic-GER reaches 9.03 B-CER **and** 3.01 CER. That isolates the contribution to the global-context loop rather than to "an LLM was involved", which is the failure mode of most post-ASR-correction papers. They also report where it does nothing (English gains are much smaller: substitutions −6.4%, deletions −0.4%, versus −21.3%/−3.0% in Chinese) and admit one configuration where overall WER rises 0.5%. Held at 7: GigaSpeechBench only, no code or prompts released, the corrections are evaluated by error rate rather than by whether a human finds the transcript more usable, the agent needs repeated re-transcription passes and no latency or cost figure is given, and the strongest results lean on Alibaba's own largest closed models — reproducibility outside the Token Foundry is unclear.

## 5. ★ Audio LLMs Know When They Can't Hear You — `2609.30625` — **Relevance 8/10 · Quality 8/10**

*Amirhosein Javadi, Richa Dixit, Mehrdad Farajtabar, Minsik Cho, Devang Naik, Mohammad Samragh* — **Apple** (first author: **UC San Diego**, internship at Apple) · cs.AI · v1 submitted 2026-09-24 · 18 pages, CC BY 4.0 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.30625)

*Problem:* When a voice query is too degraded, an Audio LLM answers a misheard question confidently, and nobody knows in advance which queries those are.
*Method:* Ask the model to self-assess (it says "reliable" almost always), then instead train a lightweight probe on the *frozen audio encoder's* representations to predict transcription reliability before any generation.
*Result:* 81.10% in-domain and 78.09% cross-domain macro-F1, beating the best baseline by 10.33 and 11.93 points; reliability labels transfer across Audio LLM families.

**Why it ranked:** Relevance 8 — Speech LLMs for ASR and understanding is Primary Focus #3, and this is the deployment-facing half of it: knowing when the modality bridge has failed. Quality 8 because the comparison set is honest and broad — **six baselines across three distinct families** (speech-quality predictors DNSMOS/NISQA/SQUIM, generation-uncertainty signals, and Fe-WER transcript-conditioned WER estimation) — and it beats the strongest of them (Fe-WER, 75.05% accuracy / 70.77% macro-F1) by 7.55 points of accuracy and 10.33 of macro-F1 while cutting MAE from 0.27 to 0.18. The negative result is the more interesting finding and they lead with it: **prompting the model to judge its own transcription barely works**, because it almost always predicts "reliable" — so the signal is present in the encoder but unavailable to introspection, which is a claim with real consequences for anyone building a clarification turn. It also carries the apparatus this kind of paper usually skips: a cross-domain split, cross-model label-bank transfer, confusion-matrix and computational-overhead analyses, and three ablations (temporal aggregation, probe depth, critical-SNR alignment) across 18 pages under CC BY 4.0. Held at 8: reliability classes are defined by synthetic corruption at estimated critical SNRs rather than by real degraded queries, the main table is a single target model (Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct), no code or label banks are announced, and the end-to-end claim — that gating on this predictor actually improves task outcomes for users — is motivated but never measured.
