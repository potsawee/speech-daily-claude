## ★ 1. When Text Misleads: Inconsistent-Aware Reasoning for Audio-Grounded Dialogue — `2608.27176` — **Relevance 9/10 · Quality 7/10**
*Yen-Ju Lu, Yuzhe Wang, Yaohan Guan, Xiluo He, Jiarui Hai, Mingrui Liang, Kaavya Chaparala, Thomas Thebaud, Laureano Moro-Velazquez, Najim Dehak, Jesus Villalba* — **Center for Language and Speech Processing, Johns Hopkins University** · cs.CL, cs.AI, cs.LG, eess.AS · 24 pages · 2026-08-27 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27176)

*Problem:* Spoken-dialogue benchmarks let a model pass on the transcript alone, so a good score never proves the prediction was grounded in the speech.
*Method:* Automatically find "cross-modal disagreement" regions — where the transcript implies a plausible but wrong reading and prosody implies a different one — and turn them into ContraTalk, 501 conflict-and-consistent questions over 117 Seamless Interaction dialogues across five discourse dimensions; then verbalise localised acoustic cues into an "Audio Twin" text representation for a reasoning model.
*Result:* Text-only LLMs exceed 90% on consistent cases and collapse to 33–48% under conflict; AudioLLMs still take the transcript-biased trap in 30–40% of conflict cases.

**Why it ranked:** ★ Followed lab (Johns Hopkins). Relevance 9 — this is your speech–text alignment question posed as a measurement: not *is the audio encoded*, but *does the model act on it when the text says otherwise*. Read next to last Friday's `2608.24958`, which found audio content legible in an audio LLM's middle layers, the pair says the loss is a routing failure rather than an encoding one — the evidence is present and the decoder overrides it. The conflict/consistent split is the design that earns the paper its keep: it separates genuine grounding from adversarial-audio dependence, so a model cannot score well by simply distrusting text. Quality 7 for breadth of coverage — seven AudioLLMs (Qwen3-Omni, KimiAudio, StepAudio-R1/2, MIMOAudio, GPT-4o-Audio-Mini among them) against six text-only baselines including Claude Opus 4.7 and DeepSeek V3.1. Held at 7 on three counts: 501 questions from 117 dialogues is small for a benchmark meant to arbitrate this claim, everything rests on a single source corpus so the "text-biased trap" distribution is Seamless Interaction's rather than speech-in-general's, and no code or data URL is posted yet — the appendix refers to a release that has not landed. The Audio Twin fix is also the weaker half: it improves conflict accuracy but its consistent-case behaviour stays backbone-dependent, which the authors say plainly.

## 2. Phoneme- and Word-Level Metrics Using Self-Supervised Speech Representations for Forced Alignment Evaluation — `2608.28508` — **Relevance 8/10 · Quality 7/10**
*V. S. D. S. Mahesh Akavarapu, Gerhard Jäger* — **University of Tübingen**; *Michael Daniel* — **University of Jena** · cs.CL · EMNLP 2026 Findings · 2026-08-28 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.28508)

*Problem:* Judging a forced aligner needs hand-annotated timestamps, which almost no language has — so alignment quality goes unmeasured exactly where it matters, in multilingual and low-resource pipelines.
*Method:* Two reference-free corpus-level metrics from SSL speech features: PCMI scores mutual information between aligned phoneme labels and clusters induced from the representations; WACS scores whether repeated words are realised consistently, via DTW similarity over their representation sequences.
*Result:* Both degrade monotonically under random and systematic misalignment and separate good from bad aligners across 85 FLEURS languages, validating against hand-annotated DoReCo alignments in 45 languages (PCMI vs. accumulated shift r ≈ −0.78).

**Why it ranked:** Relevance 8 — this is the least glamorous entry and the most likely to end up in your toolchain. Any multilingual or low-resource ASR pipeline that leans on forced alignment currently has no way to tell whether the alignment is any good in the languages where it is most likely to be bad; this gives you a number without annotation, in a pip-installable package, over ~132 languages including two phonologically hard East Caucasian ones (Archi, Rutul). Quality 7 for validating the right way round — perturbation studies establish the metrics *respond* to misalignment before any correlation is claimed, then the correlation is checked against manual timestamps rather than against another automatic aligner. Held at 7 and not higher because the metrics are corpus-level: they rank aligners and flag bad language-system pairs, they do not tell you which utterance is misaligned, so they diagnose rather than repair. WACS is also the weaker of the two (r = −0.62 to −0.67 depending on the representation model), and both inherit whatever phonetic blind spots the underlying SSL model carries — a circularity the paper acknowledges but cannot escape.

## ★ 3. Is Prosody Lost in Translation? Fine-Grained Cross-Lingual Prosody Similarity Across Languages — `2608.27848` — **Relevance 7/10 · Quality 6/10**
*Haopeng Xie, Ismail Rasim Ulgen, Sofia Son, Berrak Sisman, Philipp Koehn* — **Center for Language and Speech Processing, Johns Hopkins University** · cs.SD, cs.CL, eess.AS · EMNLP 2026 Findings · 2026-08-28 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27848)

*Problem:* Expressive speech-to-speech translation assumes prosody should carry across languages, but nobody has measured whether pitch, energy and timing patterns actually correspond between a source utterance and its translation.
*Method:* Take professionally dubbed TV entertainment across English–German, English–Spanish and English–French — roughly 16–17k timestamp-aligned utterance pairs and 35–38 hours per pair after quality, diarisation and semantic-alignment filtering — and correlate fine-grained pitch, energy and temporal contours against a shuffled baseline.
*Result:* Correlations are consistently positive and beat the shuffled control, but are weak in absolute terms — pitch 0.229–0.246, energy 0.174–0.183 — with pitch stronger and more variable than energy.

**Why it ranked:** ★ Followed lab (Johns Hopkins), and Philipp Koehn on a speech-translation paper is worth a look on its own. Relevance 7 — speech translation is Primary Focus for you, and this is the empirical groundwork under every "prosody-preserving S2ST" claim in the literature. The honest reading is the useful one, and it cuts against the framing: a pitch correlation of ~0.24 means intonational structure survives dubbing *detectably* but not *strongly*, so systems that transplant source prosody wholesale are optimising toward a target that professional human dubbers themselves only weakly hit. That is a real constraint on how much prosody transfer is even the right objective. Quality 6 rather than higher: it is analysis-only with no system, no released corpus (the dubbing data is commercial TV), and all three pairs are English paired with a Western European language — which is precisely the setting where prosodic correspondence should be *easiest*, so the weak numbers are close to a best case rather than a representative one. Professional dubbing also constrains timing to fit lip movement, which confounds the temporal features with a production artefact the paper does not fully separate out.

## ★ 4. Modality Maturity Index: A benchmark for assessing multimodal capabilities of omni models — `2608.26317` — **Relevance 6/10 · Quality 7/10**
*Rohit Patel, Dieuwke Hupkes, Sloan Strader* — **Meta Superintelligence Labs** · cs.CV, cs.AI, cs.MM · 26 pages, code and dataset released · 2026-08-26 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.26317)

*Problem:* Frontier models are sold as "omni" systems, but evaluation is almost entirely bimodal — text plus one other modality — so nothing tests whether a model can take several modalities in and return several out.
*Method:* 893 self-contained prompts spanning text, image, audio, video and document, combining up to three modalities on the input *and* the output side, each carrying human-authored per-modality rubric criteria; a supplementary Modality Presence Score computes per-prompt F1 over which output modalities the model actually returned.
*Result:* Presence alone ranges from 15.6 (Claude Opus 4.6) to 34.9 (GPT-5.4), with Gemini 3.1 Pro at 25.4 and Llama 4 Maverick at 16.3 — too few assets came back to score content quality at all.

**Why it ranked:** ★ Followed lab (Meta), and Dieuwke Hupkes has a track record of evaluation work that survives contact with the field. Relevance 6 — audio is one modality of five here, so this sits outside your ASR/alignment centre, but the audio-in/audio-out cells are the part of "omni" that touches your work, and the benchmark is one of the few that tests them jointly rather than one at a time. The finding worth carrying into a group chat is the deflationary one: the industry's omni claims fail at *presence* before correctness is even reachable, and the authors report MPS as the headline precisely because they could not compute the metric they set out to build. Quality 7 for hand-authored rubrics on every prompt and a judge validated against rubric-blind human annotators at 70.8% agreement — an unusually honest check, since the humans never saw the criteria the judge applied. Capped at 7 because MPS largely measures product surface rather than model capability: whether an API endpoint returns an image or audio asset is a deployment decision, so the ranking partly sorts vendors' generation plumbing rather than their models' multimodal understanding, and 70.8% judge–human agreement is respectable but not high enough to settle close comparisons.

## 5. LibriBrain100: One Hundred Hours of Broad and Deep MEG Data for Neural Speech Decoding at Scale — `2608.25204` — **Relevance 5/10 · Quality 9/10**
*Francesco Mantegna, Dulhan Jayalath, Gereon Elvers, Tasha Kim, Benjamin Ballyk, Alex Fung, SungJun Cho, Teyun Kwon, Luisa Kurth, Miran Özdogan, Gilad Landau, Pratik Somaiya, Natalie Voets, Mark Woolrich, Oiwi Parker Jones* — **PNPL, Department of Engineering Science, University of Oxford**; **FMRIB** and **OHBA**, Oxford Centre for Integrative Neuroimaging · cs.LG, cs.CL · 32 pages, CC-BY · 2026-08-25 · [Link](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.25204)

*Problem:* Non-invasive brain-to-text decoding is bottlenecked by data, and the field cannot tell whether to buy depth (many hours from one person) or breadth (many people), because no dataset offers both under one protocol.
*Method:* Over 100 hours of MEG recorded while subjects listened to naturalistic continuous speech — ~80 hours from a single subject, 8× the next comparable dataset and ~80× typical ones, plus ~40 minutes each from 32 further subjects — with fixed train/validation/test splits, an open Python loader, and a public competition and leaderboard.
*Result:* SOTA on word classification: ~60% top-10 balanced accuracy within-subject against 20% chance, and cross-subject fine-tuning from the deep subject lifts the 40-minute subjects by ~15 points to ~45%, holding up at a quarter of the training data.

**Why it ranked:** Relevance 5 — this is the one entry outside your stated profile, and it is here on quality, which the ranking rules allow for a standout. The reason it is worth your attention anyway: the depth-versus-breadth result is a transfer-learning finding, not a neuroscience one. Eighty hours from one subject, used as a pre-training base, buys back most of what 40 minutes per subject cannot supply — the same shape of result as speaker adaptation and low-resource ASR, in a modality where the "speaker gap" is a whole nervous system. Quality 9 is the highest score this feed has given in weeks and it is the infrastructure that earns it: fixed splits, a pip-installable loader with optional preprocessing, CC-BY licensing, and a public leaderboard released *with* the paper rather than promised — the combination that makes a dataset paper actually reproducible instead of merely citable. Not a 10 only because the headline depth rests on a single subject, so "80 hours beats 32 shallow subjects" is a claim about one nervous system, and word classification is a stepping-stone task rather than the open brain-to-text problem the abstract invokes.

Three that were genuinely in contention and did not make the cut:

- **`2608.25177` AudioLens: Multi-Perspective Speech Clustering with Reasoning Audio-Language Models** (UC Irvine, Dartmouth) — a nice task formulation: partition a speech collection according to a natural-language *perspective*, inferring both cluster count and assignments, over linguistic and paralinguistic cues at once. Held back because the benchmark, the method and the evaluation all come from the same group with no external point of comparison and no released code.
- **`2608.27817` Auditing Generative Audio Calls for Known-Task Audio-LLM Evaluation** (Mengzhe Geng, solo) — carries the sharpest negative result of the window: on VocalSound, a selector allowed to call Qwen2-Audio/Qwen2.5-Omni/MOSS-Audio reaches 0.925, while a matched selector with *all generative actions removed* reaches 0.921 (paired difference 0.004, 95% CI [−0.025, 0.033]). Supervised CLAP and WavLM probes alone get 0.850 and 0.854. If it holds, calling an audio LLM buys nothing on a known closed-set task once encoder evidence is used. One dataset and one author, so the claim is worth knowing and not yet worth believing. Its companion `2608.27783` (SURE-Challenge) is weaker — the proposed fix is a hand-tuned energy-plus-Whisper-score threshold.
